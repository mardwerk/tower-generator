# Evidence for a small Unit generation experiment

Research date: September 19, 2026. These are research findings and application proposals, not accepted product requirements. Read against Unit Generator's README, CONTEXT, PRODUCT and AUTHORING-WORKFLOW, and Towerright's PRODUCT and UNIT-DESIGN-WORKFLOW. No model calls or production changes were made.

The useful next target is one complete, readable Unit from explicit inputs. Research supports composing authored building blocks, enforcing explicit constraints and letting humans steer important choices. It does not establish that a general grammar, solver or learned model will produce compelling Tower Defense adaptations from arbitrary descriptions. Start with a small table of compatible design choices and one complete output, then measure where it fails.

## Five original papers

1. Joris Dormans, 2010, *Adventures in Level Design: Generating Missions and Spaces for Action Adventure Games*. PCGames workshop. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/1814256.1814257), [workshop PDF](https://www.pcgworkshop.com/archive/dormans2010adventures.pdf). Separates a mission's logical structure from its spatial realization, using suitable grammars for each. This is evidence for generating connected structure before its detailed realization, rather than independently filling slots. The paper explicitly says grammars are game specific and their quality requires expert design. Application proposal: connect role, basic attack, progression and dependencies before wording each upgrade. It does not demonstrate Unit generation or eliminate authoring work.

2. Adam M. Smith and Michael Mateas, 2011, *Answer Set Programming for Procedural Content Generation: A Design Space Approach*. IEEE Transactions on Computational Intelligence and AI in Games 3(3), pages 187 to 200. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCIAIG.2011.2158545), [author PDF](https://adamsmith.as/papers/tciaig-asp4pcg.pdf). Represents a design space explicitly and obtains artifacts through a general solver. Its worked reconstruction of an evolutionary generator separates possible structures from restrictions. Application proposal: represent prerequisites, incompatible combinations and confirmed decisions explicitly. ASP, CSP or SMT could eventually solve difficult combinations; this paper does not compare all three or justify adding any now. Ordinary TypeScript checks and small enumeration may suffice. A solver guarantees only encoded properties, not character fidelity, style or fun.

3. Adam M. Smith, Mark J. Nelson and Michael Mateas, 2010, *LUDOCORE: A Logical Game Engine for Modeling Videogames*. IEEE CIG. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/ITW.2010.5593368), [author PDF](https://adamsmith.as/papers/ieeecig10_ludocore.pdf). Uses explicit state/event rules to generate gameplay traces and answer temporal or structural queries. It supports incremental modeling rather than requiring a finished game at entry. Application proposal: keep missing semantics visible and add executable checks only after those semantics and tool support exist. Writing a mechanic proposal or legal upgrade graph is not equivalent to executing its behavior. This work is relevant to later validation, not a reason to build a logical runtime now.

4. Antonios Liapis, Georgios N. Yannakakis and Julian Togelius, 2013, *Sentient Sketchbook: Computer-Aided Game Level Authoring*. FDG. [Author PDF](https://antoniosliapis.com/papers/sentient_sketchbook.pdf). Combines coarse human sketches, automatic checks and search-generated alternatives using constrained novelty search and objective-based evolution. Its small study involved five experts and 24 sessions. Some suggestions were useful for early drafts or later refinements; others ignored the designer's structure or appeared unrelated. Application proposal: return one coherent draft and permit consequential revision while preserving confirmed choices. Search is worth considering only when candidate representations and useful evaluation criteria exist. Numeric fitness is not proof of quality or game-wide balance.

5. Matthew Guzdial, Nicholas Liao and Mark Riedl, 2018, *Co-Creative Level Design via Machine Learning*. Experimental AI in Games workshop. [Open-access paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09420). Studies learned partners for Super Mario Bros. level editing, with 84 retained participants, then evaluates adaptations using simulated interactions. Preferences varied, and the authors explicitly limit generalization and call for further user evaluation. They also identify the cost of obtaining suitable content and interaction data for new games. This is evidence against assuming an off-the-shelf learned generator will understand design intent. It does not test modern LLMs, Jev or TD units. Limit generative AI by measuring a bounded use, not by presuming a learned replacement is cheap.

The linked full texts were retrieved and inspected. Bibliographic metadata was cross-checked through Crossref where available; the author PDFs and arXiv record establish the remaining titles, authors and dates.

## Application proposal

For familiar mechanics, a compact authored vocabulary can select and combine attacks, progression patterns and effects without repeated generative inference. Questions translate the brief into preferences within that vocabulary. A default intake of roughly ten questions is a product hypothesis, not a research result: skip answered questions, ask unresolved identity and primary playstyle, and propose smaller choices. A known character still needs scoped source evidence; an original needs the user's intended identity. Free prose cannot reliably become exact semantics without either human interpretation, bounded AI assistance or a restricted input form.

Keep the vocabulary open to extension. A genuinely new mechanic should appear beside the draft as an unapproved proposal with its missing rules. Do not force it into a similar existing effect or silently approve it. Mechanical legality, expressive fidelity and coherent playstyle are separate evaluation questions. Generate numbers only from supplied reference values. Limited inference can shift substantial effort into templates, rules, source curation and review; measure that effort too.

The proposed product split follows task dependencies, not a conclusion proved by these papers. Unit Generator should independently create and revise one complete Unit from a brief, rules, evidence, decisions and explicit previous Result. Keep unit-level composition and checks there. Towerright adds retained project context, input revision resolution, curated Profiles, relationships and wider evaluation across units, crews and maps. A hosted project should not be required to complete the public generator's core task. CLI history stays explicit; UnitLab may retain local work.

Try one experiment before architectural expansion: use public Mira and the existing Luffy evidence, without fresh model runs. Hand-compose one compact kit from a small choice table and explicit prerequisites, preserving all confirmed decisions. Compare it with the existing drafted output, then apply one playstyle revision. For Mira, retain permanent personal wall detection and blocked Spark delivery; keep a wall-passing variant explicitly unapproved. For Luffy, check that the composed result still expresses the character rather than merely allocating techniques to slots. Record missing vocabulary, unsupported mechanics, contradictions, completeness, human corrections and total preparation/review time. If manually composing the table takes more effort than reviewing the current draft, inference savings alone do not justify automation. Add a small selector only if this experiment shows repeatable value. No generic solver, new DSL runtime, trained model or mandatory Jev integration is needed for this test.

## Follow-up: retrieval and optimization as design tools

This exploration broadens the earlier experiment beyond tables/templates. A credible engineered tool could retrieve candidate mechanics, synthesize legal whole kits and expose diverse alternatives. These operations solve different problems; they need not be alternative architectures.

Three additional primary sources, with retrieved full texts:

- Nils Reimers and Iryna Gurevych, 2019, [*Sentence-BERT: Sentence Embeddings using Siamese BERT-Networks*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084), EMNLP. Produces reusable sentence vectors for efficient similarity comparison, supporting semantic retrieval and clustering. Its similarity and transfer benchmarks do not establish mechanical equivalence, negation handling or character fidelity.
- Jean-Baptiste Mouret and Jeff Clune, 2015, [*Illuminating search spaces by mapping elites*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.04909), original MAP-Elites preprint. Retains high-performing candidates across designer-selected feature bins instead of returning only one optimum. Demonstrations concern neural networks and robots, not Units; useful features and performance measurements must still be supplied.
- Ahmed Khalifa, Scott Lee, Andy Nealen and Julian Togelius, 2018, [*Talakat: Bullet Hell Generation through Constrained Map-Elites*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.04718), GECCO. Generates projectile patterns from a grammar, combining constrained search with simulated-player evaluation. This is direct evidence for engineered attack-pattern generation. It depends on an executable representation and player model; experiments ran for 24 hours each. Playability was its fitness criterion, not character identity or demonstrated human enjoyment.

Application comparison: retrieval is the cheapest way to reuse a substantial catalog. Index individual ability claims with source/version links, then retrieve candidate attack/effect patterns and filter their explicit prerequisites. Whole-character vectors may find broad themes but can obscure minority signature abilities and incompatible story versions. Keep character-level identity constraints alongside ability-level retrieval. Similarity recommends a mapping; it never approves one.

Clustering could reveal recurring mechanic families and catalog duplication. It adds little synthesis power by itself: nearby abilities can have crucial differences in delivery, duration, target or exception scope. Its engineering burden is moderate, including representation choices and cluster review. An unmatched required behavior must remain a gap; neither low similarity nor cluster membership reliably proves novelty. Compare the requested semantic fields with supported rules and preserve unresolved differences.

Constraint-based synthesis, as in Smith and Mateas above, chooses mutually compatible parts for a whole kit. Hard constraints preserve confirmed decisions and prerequisites; soft scores can rank preferred adaptations. Modeling effort dominates solver choice. Multiobjective optimization can retain tradeoffs between fidelity, complexity and measured performance rather than hiding them in one weighted score. MAP-Elites instead emphasizes coverage across selected descriptors, such as active reliance and support emphasis, retaining quality within each bin. Neither mechanism creates a trustworthy objective. Dynamic balance needs simulation or playtesting against relevant encounters; identity and appeal need human judgments or validated preference feedback. Static proxies can support exploratory search, explicitly labeled as such.

A discriminating small experiment would extract a shared set of roughly 20 ability/effect records from existing Mira and Luffy evidence, without pooling private projects. Compare keyword/tag retrieval with ability-level embedding retrieval and whole-character retrieval, using manually marked relevant matches. Embedding runs are proposed, not performed here. Inspect clusters only for useful families and mistaken merges. Then enumerate a deliberately small set of legal complete kits from the same records, comparing the top scored kit with representatives from a tiny two-descriptor archive. This tests the value of diversity selection before implementing evolutionary MAP-Elites. Preserve Mira's perception/delivery boundary, Luffy's signature choices and one deliberately unsupported mechanic. Measure relevant retrieval, silent mismatches, identity preservation, meaningful alternatives, revision effort and setup cost. Two characters can expose failures but cannot establish generalization. Reusing an explicit catalog is distinct from training on private cross-project data; neither retrieval nor optimization requires that training.

## Jev as a selector

The user also proposes classifying Units with Jev before selecting paths. This remains an experiment. Root retrieved the [primitives](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives) and [jev-1.13 limitations](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13) on September 19, 2026. The vendor recommends narrow semantic questions with explicit options, relevant context and a fallback when no option fits. Each batched question sees the same state independently. A classification answer does not automatically become context for dependent path selection; code must supply it in a later request or evaluate complete candidates.

Supply sourced ability facts, scoped character identity, category definitions and the relevant game constraints. Do not depend on unmeasured built-in knowledge of a character. Several independent facets, such as delivery, target scope and activation, may preserve more information than a single exclusive Unit class. Treat suggested roles or themes as preferences during whole-kit composition; confirmed path choices remain binding. Add an explicit unsupported or insufficient-evidence outcome, and test cases where the closest familiar mechanic is still wrong.

Jev's documentation warns about numerical reasoning, complex indirection and irrelevant large context. It does not generate new mechanic text, and its confidence is not calibrated proof that a mapping is correct. Use code for compatibility, progression and numerical evaluation. Compare Jev with tags and embedding retrieval on the same labeled examples, including errors, latency, cost and human correction effort. No API call, integration or claim of improved design quality was made here.
